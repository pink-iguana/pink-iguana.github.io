*In November 2013, Sachin Tendulkar played his final test against the West Indies at the Wankhede. Final Test traces those fateful two and a half days, as Sachin takes to the pitch one last time.*

Dilip D'Souza's book is not a tribute to the batting maestro. If you are looking for a book that pays lip service to the legend of Tendulkar, this is not that book. In fact, he himself admits while talking about Tendulkar's legacy that "far more lucid and expressive writers" than him "will tell us about his legacy in fine detail." He also tells us that his opinions are coloured by a personal preference for fiery fast bowlers. At this point, you might as well ask, why read his book at all if it does not wax eloquent about Sachin? Because his book rises above banality.

Let me explain what I mean by this. In India, you cannot throw a stone without hitting a Sachin fan. I am one of those and I have been one of those for quite some time. Long before Sachin actually retired, I had decided to write something when he actually does, to commemorate the occasion. I used to dabble in writing at times and it gave me solace in times of grief. Sachin's retirement definitely counted as one of those times. However, when he actually retired in 2013, I could not bring myself to write about it at all. It was not as if I were too grief-stricken to write or I had stopped caring. I watched almost every ball of that final test, as the ritual demanded. Yet when the match actually ended, I was just too drained to write. What would I say that had not been said already? What words could I pen that would escape the jaws of banality?

While writing about Tendulkar, it is very easy to fall prey to the hero trope or vilify him unjustly. *Final Test* does neither. It is an honest account of the proceedings during Tendulkar's final test match from the boisterous Wankhede stands. Part of the appeal of this book is bits of cricket history peppering the narrative. Tennis stories are thrown in the mix too including those of John McEnroe who happens to be Sachin's sporting inspiration. It is surprising though that Sachin, in his long career and through all those terrible umpiring decisions, never had a McEnroe moment.

The author consciously steers himself away from romanticising the spectacle. If the author is a little too cautious in this regard, then he makes up for it by being amply wistful about the West Indies cricket team of yesteryears. There are tidbits of social commentary as well which fit nicely into the overall scheme of things. Trivia has been freely doled out. It is also interesting to note that the author has used "she" as the default pronoun.

I would recommend this book mainly because it raises an important question – is an individual greater than the sport? As in the case of Shakespeare's *Julius Caesar*, the writer does not need to highlight the greatness of the protagonist because the audience is very much aware of it. The objective of the work then is to point out the fatal flaw. Was the Final Test a compromise by one of the greatest sportsmen to play the game of cricket?
