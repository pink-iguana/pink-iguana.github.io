*A Kaula tantrik is brutally murdered by his chandaali slave. The same night, a group of university students lose their minds and perform an orchestrated orgy in front of the Vidhan Sabha metro station. To get to the truth, Anantya Tantrist, unofficial consultant with the Central Bureau of Investigation, must navigate her way past murderous sorcerers, deadly chandaalis, an underground betting scam run by jinns, and a renegade aghori teacher. As time slips through her fingers, Anantya must confront her past and rid it of its demons … before she meets a slow and painful death at the hands of her deadliest enemy yet.*

There might be minor spoilers for the first two books in the series.

This is the third instalment in the Anantya Tantrist series after *Cult of Chaos* and *The Matsya Curse*. When I first read Cult of Chaos, I loved Anantya's character but was miffed by how Shukra was portrayed. Shukra is a stereotypical genius scientist who also happens to be a descendant of Shukracharya, an ancient Asura sage. When we first meet Shukra, he refuses to believe in tantrism even though its existence is widely known after the formation of CAT (Central Association of Tantriks). The explanations offered by him for magical phenomena make very little sense and just seem like a bunch of scientific jargon strung together. I am relieved to see that the author has now decided to treat any scientific feat performed by Shukra as a black box. I was also glad to see the evolution of Shukra as a character over the next couple of books, even though I still feel that he is a bit underwritten. I also feel that as a reader there is no emotional connect with Madhu, the CBI officer. A major character in the first book, he has faded into the background and is often just there for convenience. While I understand that some characters are not so important for a particular arc, Madhu, as a half-rakshasa, can be an interesting character to explore.

The protagonist of the series is Anantya who is a tantrik. She is stubborn, reckless, kind, pugnacious, resourceful, all at once. While she lives in an alternate reality, there are some obvious parallels to our reality. For one, the people of Delhi still act the exact same way, but more importantly, the rampant misogyny within the CAT, and even outside, mirrors the problems of our own society. While I felt that in the first book there was way more "telling" than "showing" of this disparity, I am happy to report that this book strikes the balance perfectly. There were some major problems with editing in the second book (or at least in the copy that I received) but there are no such problems in this one.

Like the previous two instalments, the theme of this book is tantriks (almost all men since the CAT does not recognise female tantriks) acting without any conscience to gain power. Anantya, having been used as a tool in these power games, hates her fellow tantriks with a passion. The exception, of course, is Dhuma, her teacher. She is a delightfully eccentric character and her relationship with her pupil is the highlight of these books for me. Barby, a yakshi, is a wonderful addition to the roster as well. A gift from Qubera (since Qubera is the god-king of all yakshas), she is effervescent, moody and surprisingly useful. Another new character in this book, Zhari, is an ardha (with breasts and a penis). I look forward to reading more about her in the coming books.

As far as the narrative is concerned, it is well paced and there aren't many dull moments. The surprises don't seem shoe-horned into the story and there is a natural progression in which the characters behave. But ultimately, it's the author's imagination that buoys this story. Some of the creatures and characters have been borrowed from mythology but they have been moulded by the author's imagination and fleshed out quite distinctly. There is no long and boring exposition for the various kinds of creatures in this world as in case of some other fantasy novels. Since Anantya is already a tantrik rather than an apprentice, the standard fantasy trope of creating the world for the readers through the education of the protagonist is not followed here. The descriptions for betaals, pretas, chandaalis etc. are organically spread out across the narrative. While exposition is an effective tool for world-building, I have always admired authors that can weave the descriptions seamlessly into the story.

In conclusion, I highly recommend reading this third instalment if you enjoyed reading the first book but had some reservations about the series after the second book (as I did). My expectations for the next book in the series are quite high. I am hoping for Anantya to grow in maturity. At present, it seems that she comes up with suicidal plans and eventually sees them through unscathed. There have to be repercussions, some collateral damage that affects her deeply even if there is no harm to her person. I am also hoping to read more about Zaazee, my favourite telly-bingeing serpent. Finally, I desperately want to know more about Shukra's past because he is the designated sidekick in this series.
