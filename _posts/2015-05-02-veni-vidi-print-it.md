*While you may have heard about 3D printing revolutionising the way we create, there is a more intriguing question – will it change what we create or what we can create?*

"Life is short, and the Art long; the occasion fleeting; experience fallacious, and judgment difficult." What is considered to be the first aphorism of Hippocrates, the father of western medicine, is also a reflection of the sceptical demeanour of the physicians at the time. They were passive and believed in self-regeneration, the healing power of nature. As it happens, modern medicine is also moving towards regenerative methods. While it may seem retrospective, the difference is that now we believe that we can cultivate organs in a laboratory or artificially induce regeneration in the human body; which is to say, physicians may soon face a three-pronged conundrum – repair, transplant or replace?

Well, I may have allowed myself to get carried away by stating the above and *soon* seems a bit of an exaggeration, but I will lay my cards on the table. Let us consider the Stone Spray project. Stone Spray is an on-site robotic 3D printer that creates architecture out of the soil. While they are still creating walls and stools out of sand, they envision creating bigger structures like bridges in remote areas out of material available in the site vicinity using just a 3D printer. And if we may allow our imagination to take a leap of faith, we can almost compare it to Doctor Manhattan (of the graphic novel *Watchmen* by Alan Moore) creating an enormous crystalline clockwork ship out of the Martian soil to travel through the Martian atmosphere. If this seems preposterous, let me put it into perspective. The European Space Agency (ESA) (with its industrial partners) is testing ways to 3D print a lunar base from lunar regolith. So instead of paper, they have simulated lunar material mixed with magnesium oxide; the ink being a salt that acts as a binder and turns the mixture into a stone-like solid mass. Monolite, the company which is responsible for providing the printer, is already printing artificial coral reefs to preserve beach ecosystems from destructive waves. And they claim that their printers would be able to raise a building on lunar soil within a week.

While we may only speculate about 3D printing wonders of the future, we are certainly recreating relics of the past using 3D printers. For instance, Materialise, an additive manufacturing (a fancy term for 3D printing and related technologies) company, built a replica of Tutankhamun's mummy (the treasure-sick boy pharaoh) using stereolithography which is a printing technique in which layers of ultraviolet curable material are stacked over each other to fabricate an object. In fact, in 2008, Ford Motor Company printed a full scaled prototype of a 77 million-year-old duckbill dinosaur, Leonardo, using *mammoth* stereolithography machines. And this had real paleontological value rather than mere extravagance because Leonardo was a rare find with a fossilised skin that had kept its internal organs intact. Damaging such an artefact by using standard mould and cast techniques was certainly not an option.

However, it is not just about replicating skeletons of long-lost creatures. There are extremely intriguing creatures in inaccessible habitats that we know nothing about. So if you come across a deep sea fish specimen that has a lower jaw with no skin at all, you may wonder how it swallows, because everything would fall right through its jaws! And yet, this stoplight loosejaw (dragonfish) that I am describing here, exists in a region where there is a remarkable scarcity of food due to very little sunlight. These also happen to be one of the only few fishes that produce red bioluminescence (in other words, they produce red light). Furthermore, they use a chlorophyll derivative to perceive red light! To study these creatures in their natural habitat is almost impossible and even creating such an environment in a lab is not easy. So what's the obvious thing to do? You print inanimate replicas of these using artificial bones, muscles, skin and tendons, and study them. This is exactly what Christopher Kenaley, a postdoctoral research associate at the University of Washington, is up to.

Here is where we allow our imagination to run amuck. What more does it take to make animated replicas of a dragonfish or a dinosaur *or a Tutankhamun*? Is it not a natural extension of regenerative medicine?

While it's easy to print layers of living cells to create tissues, it is difficult to develop a vascular system along with it to sustain the tissue. Organovo, a company that deals in bioprinting, has been able to overcome this hurdle to a certain degree. To print a human liver tissue, printing hepatocytes, that are the chief functional cells of liver, is not enough. Organovo's researchers brought together fibroblasts (connective tissue) and endothelial cells (cells that line the inner surface of blood vessels) that allowed the development of tiny vascular networks. Does this mean we'll get 3D printed organs in the near future? Not really. An entire organ is an extremely complex affair, to the extent that some even deny that an exact replica of a human organ is possible anytime in the future (including Professor Gabor Forgacs, around whose technology Organovo is formed). Why then is printing a human liver tissue (and keeping it fully functional for 40 days, as in this case) such a big deal? Well, it eliminates the need of using animals in pre-clinical trials of a drug and also provides a more accurate way of determining the potency and toxicity of the drug, eventually saving more than half a billion dollars. But it's not just that. Some people are actually hopeful that organ printing is round the corner.

Researchers in Edinburgh (Heriot-Watt University) have created printers that print living embryonic stem cells and retain their functionality. Now, stem cells have this ability to differentiate into specialised cells such as those present in the organs. Hence they believe that this technology could evolve into something which could enable organ printing.

Imagine printers spitting out organs on demand and making the idea of organ transplant obsolete. If that does not seem hysterical enough, we could as well think of assembly lines for customised pets and animals. And why stop at real animals? Can't I print imaginary creatures like a chimaera or a dragon? Of course, it does raise ethical issues, but for once, I would not mind if I could just do such a thing once.

But 3D printing does come with its associated caveats. For instance, one cannot regulate 3D printing of firearms. With 3D printers becoming increasingly cost effective, printing a firearm will be incredibly easy. In 2013, a US-based group, Defense Distributed, came up with a working blueprint to print a plastic gun. They have also succeeded in designing a 3D printable rifle and a 30 round M16 magazine (M16 is a common American assault rifle). With options like metal printing becoming more accessible, various other kinds of firearms will also become 3D printable.

I guess I am overdoing this. I have harped on too much about 3D printing. But since I have already blurred fantasy and fact, I will entreat you to consider one more crazy idea. 4D printing.

Does it even mean anything?

Let me explain. When you 3D print an object, it's stationary. Assembly of 3D printed objects into a complex object is like imparting intelligence to the system. In this case, this is done externally. Can we directly print this intelligence into the system so that it assembles itself? Apparently, we can. In a collaboration between Stratasys' Education, R&D departments and MIT's Self-Assembly Lab, research is going on to develop materials that could self-assemble. They have developed strands that self-assemble into cubes when dropped in water (water provides the activation energy for local interaction). It is akin to self-assembly of biomolecules like lipids to form membranes or DNA to form a double helix. But while this occurs at a molecular level, this research focuses on allowing self-assembly on a human scale.

While 3D printing is touted as a disruptive technology, it is also expected to change the way we create. With concepts like 4D printing, does it also mean that eventually, the "we" will be taken out of the equation (the way objects create *themselves*)? So if I may beat a tactical retreat, I might as well drop the idea of an assembly line for printing animates. It seems redundant.
