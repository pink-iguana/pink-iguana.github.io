Obviously, this title is clickbait. The question, as put forth here, is not [well-posed](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/Well-posed_problem). It is not clear if an answer to this question exists at all, and even if it does, it is certainly not unique. Unlike how mathematicians are mostly in straitjackets in [pop culture](https://philarchive.org/archive/PANTGG), we actually come in all shapes and sizes. Let me reframe the question then: What do I, as a number theorist, do and want in life? The first part of this question is relatively easy to answer, and I will do so in the first couple of posts in this series. The second question, though, is rather open-ended, and one might argue that it is still not well-posed. I don't want this to be an excuse for waffling, so, instead, I will choose to interpret it as what I want from my professional life. Since we are on LinkedIn, I suppose you should have seen this coming from a mile away.

### What do I do?

I investigate patterns in numbers for a living. This might seem rather self-indulgent and sometimes it is, but mostly, it is not. [Cryptography](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/27967000.pdf), famously, is a by-product of such investigations. To a number theorist, though, the mathematics underpinning the modern cryptographic protocols is old news. The RSA cryptosystem, which is still widely used, goes back to the 1970s but the [problem of integer factorisation](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/ssw/chapter3.pdf) it's based on goes back at least to Eratosthenes, 2500 years ago. This is not to say that research in cryptography is stale, but it can't be the only poster child of number theory, a branch of mathematics that was described by Gauss as the queen of mathematics. I don't do number theory because cryptography exists. I do number theory because it deals with primes and in mathematics, only primes are real; everything else is a social construct. Though I admit that last bit was quite wanky, let me have this because this is as close to a mic drop as I am going to get in my line of work. My research is mostly concerned with finding and proving non-trivial patterns in the seemingly random world of (prime) numbers. It involves a lot of staring at the blackboard, mostly because many in the field are yet to adopt whiteboards, and personally, I like to watch wet chalk dry. It is less time intensive than watching paint dry and the excitement associated with ghostly symbols apparating minutes after being drawn is real. Simple atavistic pleasures of life. But there are also more avant-garde pleasures to be had in number theory. I do make use of some serious computational power in my research. NCI Australia's shiny new supercomputer, Gadi, lets me (partially) verify number theory conjectures in hours rather than the months it would have taken on my friendly household PC, which goes by the name *Desk Iguana*. The reasons for it being named thus are myriad but suffice it to say that I think of it as a silicon-based reptile.

While silicon-based reptiles might well breathe mathematics, I am just a lowly [carbon-based reptile](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/Reptilian_conspiracy_theory). I mostly breathe the fumes from the wreckage of another of my attempts at solving a problem. That is the nature of research most days. Even on days when something does work out, the closest I come to [Archimedes](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/fact-or-fiction-archimede/) is when I take a long shower to reflect, in quiet satisfaction, on the arduous process I went through. Research in number theory and more broadly, research in mathematics, is often about chipping away at a problem even when it seems overwhelming at first. To solve a problem, you first need to read some of the existing mathematical literature and approach the problem with these acquired tools. If these tools don't suffice, you read more and try to scale the problem again. This process is repeated until the problem is solved or it becomes clear that the problem is intractable using currently available technology. At this point in time, depending upon what you want out of a problem in terms of your career, you either decide to get your head down and develop the necessary tools on your own or you hold your head high knowing that you tried what you could but because of your current goals, you don't want to spend more time on the problem. And boy, there are some rabbit holes out there.

If you think the mystery of Jack the Ripper is enduring, try the [Goldbach conjecture](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-32162-2_16) on for size. I have often waxed lyrical about this problem at parties. My exploits in this area include convincing at least one philosopher to study mathematics more deeply during the course of a birthday party. On a completely unrelated note, I have not interacted with this person again. I think they are studying mathematics every waking moment and have no time for human interaction. My persistence paid off. I am quite persistent when it comes to solving problems too, even when certain aspects of these problems might require some grunt work. The amount of grunt work depends on the area of mathematics you work in. It is not something that we like to brag about but the focus of our [research group](https://www.unsw.adfa.edu.au/our-research/number-theory) is explicit number theory which requires a fair bit of grunt work. Before grunt and explicit give you any NSFW ideas, I should clarify that explicit, here, means that we provide the numbers (computations) in number theory. The more exalted minds in the area stick to the theory part of number theory.

So, how do I go about putting the numbers where they belong, er, in number theory? This will be addressed in my next post where I will discuss the Goldbach conjecture and how it relates to my current research. More importantly, I will also give you tips on how to cheat in mathematics.

### Cheating in mathematics

One of my recent projects was related to the Goldbach conjecture–something I mentioned in the first post of this series. To quickly explain what the conjecture is all about, consider this pattern: 4 can be written as the sum of 2 and 2, where 2 is a prime. 6, similarly, can be written as the sum of 3 and 3, 3 again being a prime. 8 is 3 plus 5–both primes. 10 is 5 plus 5, 12 is 5 plus 7, and so on. From this, one might expect that every even number greater than or equal to 4 can be written as a sum of two prime numbers. This expectation is known as the Goldbach conjecture.

It has a lot of computational heft behind it because we [know](https://www.ams.org/journals/mcom/2014-83-288/S0025-5718-2013-02787-1/S0025-5718-2013-02787-1.pdf) it to be true for all even numbers up to 4×10¹⁸. This is a big number; while I don't want to use the tired old comparison with the number of sand grains on our planet, just for reference, it is the length of 3.33×10¹⁶ American football fields in yards. As my PhD supervisor, Tim Trudgian would say: I hope that answers all your questions. Anyway, so the Goldbach conjecture is true? Not so fast, all you physicists. The Goldbach conjecture is true up to 4×10¹⁸. That's awfully pedantic! How long do we have to wait until we can make sweeping conclusions about the nature of spacetime? We have to wait until we can verify this for every last one of the countably infinite even numbers greater than or equal to 4. But that's impossible – there are an infinite number of them! Precisely. More verification is good and can lend more support to the validity of the conjecture but can never prove it. We need to find a clever workaround. So what's the clever workaround? We don't have one yet. Ah, not so clever. But, but, we have a clever approximation. What if, instead of writing an even number as a sum of two primes, we write it as a sum of two primes plus a few other things? That's cheating! Well, at least the physicists are back on board. You see, when we mock physicists for existing in an [eleven-dimensional world](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/M-theory) where mathematical rigour does not exist, we forget that when we can't take the heat, we also resort to subterfuge in the form of approximations. So yes, approximating is cheating and cheating is okay, even in mathematics, as long as you can quantify your cheating. If you don't know how much you are cheating, you could be cheating too much and that's not okay. In our case, those few other things that we add to the two primes are a few powers of two. Why powers of two? Because we don't want to cheat too much and the powers of two are so dispersed that it ensures that we are not cheating too much. In the first 100 natural numbers, there are 50 even numbers, 25 prime numbers, and only 6 powers of two, or in other words, 5% the length of an American football field in yards. As we go higher, the powers of two only get sparser. Therefore, by adding only a tiny sliver of the natural number buffet to the already measly portion of primes, we want to be able to represent all even numbers. This approximation was first figured out by the Soviet mathematician Yuri Linnik, who showed that we can write every (sufficiently large) even number as a sum of two primes and a finite number of powers of two.

Now, if, like me, you are into explicit stuff, that word finite should be mildly irritating. You want an actual number in there for the number of powers of two. Thankfully, in the last few decades, various mathematicians have worked this out and the [state of the art](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10474-020-01077-8.pdf) for this problem is 8 powers of two. Not bad at all. We now know that every (sufficiently large) even number is the sum of two primes and at most 8 powers of two. Notice that I have added the rider "sufficiently large" a couple of times already in this discussion. Why am I acting so weaselly all of a sudden? What sort of dodgy law firm do I run from my garage? I hereby admit to cheating but only as much as every other mathematician. This is a classic mathematician's cheat code. If we can't prove interesting properties without eliminating a few low-lying numbers (think billions of trillions), we will employ the phrase "for a sufficiently large number". Often, it's a limitation posed by currently available computing power and/or algorithms. So really, it's the fault of the computer scientists (aka glorified mathematicians with enough money to have their own department).

What do I have to do with all of this? Before we get to that, we need to talk about Joseph-Louis Lagrange. Lagrange's contributions to mathematics and the sciences are very many but the work that we are concerned with is his theorem of four squares. It states that every natural number can be written as the sum of four perfect squares (including 0). As an example, consider 15, which can be written as 9+4+1+1, where 9, 4, and 1 are perfect squares. Lagrange proved this theorem in 1770 and since then, mathematicians have asked more difficult questions (as they always do) along similar lines. For instance, is there more than one way of writing a number as a sum of four squares? Not for 15, but 16 (which itself is a perfect square) can be written as 16+0+0+0 and 4+4+4+4. A natural question then is to ask how many such representations exist for a given integer. This question was [answered](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/Jacobi%27s_four-square_theorem) by Jacobi. In a slightly different direction, we can ask if all even numbers can be written as the sum of four squares of primes à la the Goldbach conjecture. The question is reasonable because although primes are relatively sparse in the set of natural numbers, there seem to be enough of them to represent all even numbers as sums of two primes. Immediately, an example and a counterexample come to mind: 16 can be written as 2²+2²+2²+2² (2 is a prime) but 8 cannot be written as the sum of four squares of primes. So, trivially, the answer is no; a bit anti-climactic but we might be on to something. If we just work a bit harder, we can unearth an interesting question. How about the old trick where we substitute all numbers with all sufficiently large numbers? Some elementary number theory will show that this is still not good enough. This is because the remainder of the square of every odd prime when divided by 8 is 1. Similarly, the remainder of the square of every prime (except 3) when divided by 3 is 1. Hence, adding four squares of primes should give the remainder 4 when the sum is divided by 24. So, clearly, not all even numbers can be represented as the sum of four squares of primes; probably only those that leave the remainder 4 when divided by 24. Collating all this information, we finally form the following question: can we write every sufficiently large even number that leaves the remainder 4 when divided by 24 as the sum of four squares of primes?

Can we? Um… we don't know. What?! Was all this just a wild goose chase? Firstly, do not go around chasing wild geese. These [aren't](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xv9UZEOFsVw) animals you want to mess with. Secondly, cheating is always an option. We have an approximate answer to the question. In 1999, Liu, Liu, and Zhan proved that we can write all sufficiently large even numbers as the sum of four squares of primes and a finite number of powers of two. Since then, a number of mathematicians have worked out the upper limit on the number of powers of two. In 2014, Lilu Zhao proved that we need at most 46 powers of two and in the following year, Dave Platt and Tim Trudgian proved that we need at most 45 powers of two. This is where I finally make an appearance. Towards the end of last year, I proved that at most 31 powers of two are required for any such representation. Take that, all you fantastic and beautiful mathematicians before me!

If you must know anything about research in mathematics, know this–it is not a Dan Brown novel. Saying something silly like "Take that!" would make you a Dan Brown mathematician, not an actual one. Heck, half of those mathematicians I mentioned in the preceding paragraph are in the acknowledgements section of my paper. The public perception of mathematicians is that of solitary and often eccentric savants operating in a vacuum, brandishing their pen and paper against a backdrop of such sheer austerity that it puts any hospital ward to shame. The truth, in fact, is that modern research mathematics is incredibly collaborative and a mathematical conference might well be a tea party for gregarious raccoons. The collaborative spirit is quite genuine as well, in that in number theory and many other areas of pure mathematics, we have done away with the political games involved with being the first, second, or nth author when we put our names on a research paper–we go alphabetically. Each author is as important as any other.

So while I felt chuffed upon proving the best result so far in this area, the only feeling I had towards the bevy of mathematicians before me was gratitude. Also, eventually, someone is going to take me down as well. Being KO'ed is just part of the job.

### What do I want?

If the *circle method* sounds like some arcane technique that was devised more than a century ago to do amazing things that were never done before, it is because it is exactly that. In additive number theory which deals with problems like the Goldbach conjecture, it is one of the most potent tools that we have at our disposal. Hardy and Ramanujan, the mathematical duo that you might know from the 2015 movie *The Man Who Knew Infinity*, invented this method in 1918 to solve a major problem in the theory of integer partitions. Since then, Hardy and Littlewood ([my academic ancestor](https://www.mathgenealogy.org/id.php?id=145274)) went to town with this method and so it is often referred to as the *Hardy–Littlewood method*. However, the modern treatment is due to the Soviet mathematician Vinogradov. If we were to be fair to all parties, we should call it the *Hardy–Littlewood–Ramanujan–Vinogradov method* but human lives are generally not long enough to get through that entire name. Even the name circle method does not make much sense now because although there were circles in the original formulation of the method, the modern formulation has nothing to do with circles. However, in order to finish writing this article in my own lifetime, I will stop being pedantic and just refer to it as the circle method.

The circle method translates a counting problem like the number of representations of an integer as the sum of a bunch of primes into the estimation of an integral. We then chop up this integral into *major arcs* and *minor arcs*. Ideally, the contribution from the major arcs should outweigh the contribution from minor arcs. This is when the circle method works. But minor arcs don't always behave. To put this in terms of the Anna Karenina principle–all major arcs are alike; each minor arc is messy in its own way. For instance, when we try to solve the Goldbach conjecture using this technique, the minor arcs play spoilsport. However, in other Goldbach-like problems such as the one I have the best result for (see the second post in this series), you can tame them just enough to get a result. In my case, the taming of the minor arcs was quite successful. More precisely, in the estimation of the minor arcs, there is a certain constant that needs to be computed. I was able to estimate this constant better than anyone else before using an algorithm that I devised for this purpose. This computation is not easy because, from an algorithmic perspective, it is [equivalent](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/Discrete_logarithm) to breaking our standard cryptographic protocols. The fact that I am not swimming in money should indicate that I did not come up with an algorithm to break cryptography. Instead, the algorithm requires you to impose certain limits on the numbers involved in the computation to make it tractable. These limits need to be justified theoretically but with enough coaxing, the numbers oblige.

The obvious takeaway from the preceding passage is that I am not swimming in money. [Money is handy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fZuqoCYdVQk) and I would not mind swimming in it but I don't want my professional life to be purely mercenary. I imagine my professional life to be like the Pacific Ring of Fire where different ideas collide, often in a spectacular fashion. Even though I am, what you would call, a "pure mathematician", most of my work is at the intersection of number theory and computer science, as indicated earlier in this article. This was a conscious choice on my part. Hybrid fields of study are like fault lines between tectonic plates. Straddling them is perilous but can be quite rewarding too. If you want to witness the seismic changes in our civilisation, this is where you want to be. It is not to say that the interior of a tectonic plate is completely inert but changes there might occur on a geologic time scale. The advantage of knowing mathematics is that it provides the necessary grounding when venturing outwards from the interior of a tectonic plate. I could keep going with this metaphor but I think if I stretch it any further, geologists of the past epochs will rise from their stratified rocky graves and come to haunt me for the rest of the Anthropocene.

So, to conclude this article, I like being a researcher in number theory but that's not all I want to be. I want to do similar things outside my stomping grounds and my hope is that this exposition illustrates that I have the guts necessary to graze in the nearby meadows.
